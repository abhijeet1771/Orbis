## Q1. Surveys and taxonomies; precision and recall of static, dynamic, history-based and hybrid CIA

### Takeaway
Thirty years of CIA research have never produced a technique whose impact sets are both complete and precise on real code. Measured against execution-based or developer-based truth, single techniques sit around 40-60% precision and 25-60% recall; combining sources helps by 10-40 percentage points, but no study reports recall near 100%. Teslon's "never a wrong skip" promise is therefore stronger than anything the CIA literature has demonstrated, and can only be defended by being conservative (over-approximating, holding UNCLEAR) and by publishing measured miss rates, not by claiming precise impact sets.

- Hassan and Holt (ICSM 2004), "Predicting change propagation in software systems": compared heuristics (historical co-change, code structure/call-use-define, code layout/same file, developer) for predicting which entities change together; historical co-change had the best balance; structural heuristics had low recall. ### Inferences
- No CIA technique in thirty years of literature reports recall near 1.0 against independent truth; the high numbers people quote (e.g. ROSE's 64% top-3 hit rate) are ranking metrics, not completeness. Teslon's TEST+UNCLEAR set must therefore be deliberately over-approximate, and its marketing should never say "precise impact set".
- History mining (Zimmermann, Ying, Hassan-Holt, TARMAQ) is the literature's main tool for cross-language and non-code coupling. It fits Teslon only as a veto (raise SKIP to UNCLEAR), never as a proof, and Rex shows it can be done deterministically and offline.
- Hybrids help ranking by 10-40 points (Gethers 2012) but none prove completeness; Teslon gains nothing for safety by adding more heuristic sources, only for ordering.
- The field's own evaluation weakness (small subjects, co-change truth, no shared benchmark) is the same weakness Teslon has; Teslon publishing a cross-stack PR-impact benchmark with external truth would be a genuine contribution.

## Q2. Safety of RTS and what breaks it

### Takeaway
"Safe" in the literature is a conditional, formal property (Rothermel and Harrold 1996): a selector is safe if, under stated assumptions, it omits no test that could reveal a fault in the modified program. Every serious study since has found that real static selectors violate safety once the assumptions are broken by reflection, non-code inputs, configuration, or plain tool bugs. The best static result (STARTS, class level) was "comparable to dynamic" and had "very few" violations, all from reflection, on 22 open-source Java libraries; that is the ceiling of what static RTS has shown, and those subjects are far simpler than a Spring + Angular enterprise app with DI, AOP, Kafka and config. A tool that claims "never a wrong skip" must therefore (a) print its assumptions next to every skip, and (b) hold everything that breaks them as UNCLEAR, which Teslon's design already does in principle.

### Cited Findings
Definitions
- Rothermel and Harrold (IEEE TSE 1996), "Analyzing regression test selection techniques". Four criteria for comparing RTS techniques: inclusiveness, precision, efficiency, generality. A technique is safe when inclusiveness is 1: "under certain well-defined conditions, [it] exclude[s] no tests (from the original test suite) that if executed would reveal faults in the modified software". — [UNL repository PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/17237569.pdf) [S]. The "well-defined conditions" (from memory [Mem]) are the controlled regression testing assumption: same environment, same inputs, deterministic tests, and the test suite's outcome on the old version is known and correct (P passes T). For Teslon: a SKIP proof is only as good as the stated conditions; Teslon must list them (profile, deployment, flags, dependency versions, no reflection/DI-by-name in the path, deterministic data) per skip. Flaky behaviour, environment drift and database state all violate the controlled assumption and are outside any static proof.
- Rothermel and Harrold (TOSEM 1997), "A safe, efficient regression test selection technique" (DejaVu, control-flow-graph walk); Harrold et al. (OOPSLA 2001), "Regression test selection for Java software" (Java interclass graph handling inheritance, dynamic binding, exceptions). [Mem; listed for lineage; not re-checked]. For Teslon: the classical safe algorithms required a complete graph of the whole program including libraries; any edge the graph lacks (framework callbacks, DI, reflection) silently breaks safety. This is the theoretical root of Teslon's "unknown site" ledger.

Empirical safety of static RTS
- Legunsen, Hariri, Shi, Lu, Zhang, Marinov (FSE 2016), "An extensive study of static regression test selection in modern software evolution", pp. 583-594, DOI 10.1145/2950290.2950361. 985 revisions of 22 open-source projects; class-level static RTS was comparable to Ekstazi (dynamic, class level) in savings "but was sometimes unsafe"; method-level static RTS "performed rather poorly"; authors' slides: STARTS "had very few safety violations". — [Authors' PDF](https://mir.cs.illinois.edu/~marinov/publications/LegunsenETAL16StaticRTS.pdf) ; [abstract](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/35355) [S]. From Teslon's council notes [Sec]: every missed test came from reflection. For Teslon: coarse beats fine for safety. Teslon's fine (endpoint, screen, field) claims are much finer than "class level", which is where the literature found static analysis "rather poor" for method level. Fine-grained skips need extra justification (change-kind proofs, below).
- Shi, Hadzi-Tanovic, Zhang, Marinov, Legunsen (OOPSLA 2019), "Reflection-aware static regression test selection", DOI 10.1145/3360613. Static RTS "can be sometimes unsafe, missing to select some tests that dynamic RTS selects, and reflection was the only cause of unsafety among the evaluated projects" (805 revisions, 22 projects, per the related Illinois thesis); purely static reflection handling restored safety but cost much of the savings; a hybrid ran at 75.8% of RetestAll end-to-end time. — [OOPSLA page](https://2019.splashcon.org/details/splash-2019-oopsla/68/Reflection-Aware-Static-Regression-Test-Selection) ; [Illinois thesis record](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/107148) [S]; Teslon council [Sec] gives 85.8% (static-safe) vs 69.1% (unsafe) of RetestAll time. Note: the brief calls this "Shi et al. ISSTA 2019"; the paper is OOPSLA 2019. For Teslon: making static analysis safe for reflection erases most of the savings; in a Spring app, reflection is everywhere (DI, proxies, Jackson, JPA). Teslon must either model each framework's reflective idiom exactly (as data, tested) or hold.
- Gyori, Legunsen, Hariri, Marinov (ISSRE 2018, not ISSTA), "Evaluating regression test selection opportunities in a very large open-source ecosystem", pp. 112-122. Maven Central: 13,961 change sets in 168 libraries with 580,876 clients; class-level RTS variants select on average only 7.8%-17.4% of the tests project-level selection does; notes Facebook, Google and Microsoft use project-level (module-level) selection. — [PDF](https://mir.cs.illinois.edu/gyori/pubs/issre18.pdf) ; [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10110972) [S]. For Teslon: dependency bumps reach enormous client sets; industry stays at module level because finer cross-library reasoning is hard to make safe. Teslon's "dependency bump -> modules that declare it" coarse rule is consistent with industry practice; anything finer for library bumps needs proof.
- Zhu, Legunsen, Shi, Gligoric (ICSE 2019), "A framework for checking regression test selection tools" (RTSCheck): found 27 bugs (23 new) in Ekstazi, STARTS and Clover by generating program evolutions and checking rules. — [GitHub](https://github.com/EngineeringSoftware/rtscheck) [Sec: Teslon council R3/R7]. For Teslon: tool bugs are a real miss source independent of theory; RTSCheck-style differential checking is the accepted method to find them.
- Liu, Nie, Legunsen, Gligoric et al. (ISSTA 2023), "More precise regression test selection via reasoning about semantics-modifying changes" (FineEkstazi/FineSTARTS): change-kind classification skips provably behaviour-preserving changes; evaluated on 1,150 versions of 23 projects; 41.7% / 31.8% fewer tests with no safety loss [Sec: council R3]; abstract notes "recent RTS techniques ... trade precision for speed ... or even use machine learning to trade safety for speed". — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10467385-more-precise-regression-test-selection-via-reasoning-about-semantics-modifying-changes) [S]. For Teslon: the strongest published pattern for "skip with proof" is a closed catalogue of change kinds proven harmless, not reachability alone. The council also notes 11 of 13 harmless-looking changes in that study were in fact behaviour-changing [Sec], a warning for any whitespace/rename/annotation "no-op" classifier.
- Shin et al. (JSS 2022) compared RTS tools' safety: OpenClover had the most violations, STARTS about equal to Ekstazi; selected subsets found 8.75% fewer faults [Sec: council R3; not re-checked]. For Teslon: an independent comparison found real fault-detection loss even with "safe" tools.
- Orso, Shi, Harrold (FSE 2004), "Scaling regression testing to large software systems": two-phase partitioning then selection for Java [S: listed in search results](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/fac/Alex.Orso/papers/orso.shi.harrold.FSE04.pdf). For Teslon: the coarse-then-fine two-phase structure is the classical way to stay safe at scale.

Configuration, environment and non-code inputs
- Wang, Lian, Marinov, Xu (ICSE 2023), "Test selection for unified regression testing" (uRTS): no prior RTS reasoned about code and configuration changes together; uRTS keeps the same safety guarantee for code-only and config-only changes and adds code+config co-changes; built on Ekstazi plus Ctest; a 2025 review reports 3.64x faster than full testing and 1.87x faster than existing RTS on five large open-source projects. — [ICSE page](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2023/icse-2023-technical-track/59/Test-Selection-for-Unified-Regression-Testing) ; [slides](https://shuaiwang516.github.io/slides/uRTS_icse23_pdf.pdf) [S]; 3.64x [Sec: arXiv 2506.16101 review]. For Teslon: config keys must be graph nodes on equal footing with code; a code-only reader is unsafe for config changes by construction.
- Sun, Cheng, Chen, Ang, Legunsen, Xu (OSDI 2020), "Testing configuration changes in context to prevent production failures" (ctest), pp. 735-751: configuration-induced failures are a major source of production incidents; cites Google, where faulty configurations were the second-largest cause of service disruptions in one major service; ctests detect failure-inducing config changes before deployment, evaluated on real failure-inducing changes (a snippet mentions 64 real-world cases, unconfirmed), injected misconfigurations, and Docker image configs. — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/sun) ; [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10293057) [S]. For Teslon: YAML/properties changes are high-risk, not low-risk; a config change Teslon cannot resolve to a reader must be UNCLEAR, and "config value changed only in a profile not deployed" needs the deployment fact, not an assumption.
- Flakiness: Shi et al. (ISSRE 2019) found 38 of 39 CI failures missed by RTS were flaky failures [Sec: council R3]. Leong et al. 2019 (Google) found flaky transitions significantly distort RTS evaluation [S, see Q4]. For Teslon: any history-based validation of skips must filter flaky failures, or it will count noise as misses.


### Inferences
- The literature's safest static selectors work at class/module granularity on single-language Java libraries. Teslon makes much finer claims (one screen, one endpoint) across Java, TypeScript, YAML, SQL migrations and messaging. Nothing published shows that granularity can be safe statically; Teslon's certificate-plus-checker design is the right shape, but it is unproven at that granularity until measured.
- Spring is reflection-heavy (DI, proxies, Jackson, JPA, AOP). Given that reflection was the sole cause of static RTS misses, each Spring idiom Teslon models is a safety-critical rule that needs its own probe set; each idiom it does not model must produce an unknown site.
- Configuration and dependency changes are the two change classes where research found static code analysis structurally blind (uRTS, ctest, Gyori). Teslon reads YAML and Maven, which puts it ahead of classic RTS, but the burden is to show config keys resolve to readers per profile, and to hold when they do not.

### Gaps
- Exact unsafety counts for STARTS (Legunsen 2016) and the unsafe percentage in Shi 2019 were not visible; the council's 85.8%/69.1% figures are secondary.
- The "controlled regression testing assumption" wording was not confirmed in a snippet; stated from memory.
- No study of RTS safety for Spring/JPA/Kafka enterprise apps or for Angular/Vue front ends was found.

## Q3. Web apps, UI, microservices, end-to-end tests, database schema and configuration

### Takeaway
Research on mapping a code change to affected screens or UI flows is thin and mostly small-scale: hybrid JavaScript impact analysis (Tochal 2015), a few web RTS case studies (PHP apps, WebKit, a 2019 Japanese industrial study for manual E2E tests), and early microservice CIA prototypes (Cerny et al. 2025). Database schema impact analysis is the most mature non-code area (Maule et al. 2008: zero false negatives on one commercial CMS case). No study found evaluates a static, cross-stack (front end + back end + DB + messaging) change-to-screen mapping at enterprise scale. Teslon is in largely unoccupied territory, which is both its novelty and the reason there is no external benchmark to lean on.

### Cited Findings
Web and UI
- Alimadadi, Mesbah, Pattabiraman (ECOOP 2015), "Hybrid DOM-sensitive change impact analysis for JavaScript" (Tochal): static analysis alone misses impacts through DOM events, event handlers registered at runtime and asynchronous client-server calls; hybrid (static + dynamic) found impacts the static-only analysis missed. [Mem for numbers; tool: https://github.com/saltlab/tochal per council R3]. For Teslon: Angular/Vue impact through events, router guards, interceptors and async HTTP is exactly where static-only CIA was measured to miss; each such hop needs a modelled idiom or UNCLEAR.
- Nakagawa, Munakata, Yamamoto (ISSRE Workshops 2019), "Applying modified code entity-based regression test selection for manual end-to-end testing of commercial web applications": applies "a simple, safe" RTS approach to manual E2E testing of legacy web apps in two development projects; it "reduced testing more precisely than a method based on developer experience", with low deployment and labour cost. — [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.cn/pub/5e524e473a55acaacc56083b/applying-modified-code-entity-based-regression-test-selection-for-manual-end-to) [S; numbers not visible]. - Beszedes et al. Database schema
- Maule, Emmerich, Rosenblum (ICSE 2008), "Impact analysis of database schema changes", pp. 451-460, DOI 10.1145/1368088.1368150. Dataflow analysis enumerates the DB interactions an OO application can make, program slicing shrinks the analysed program; needs more context-sensitivity than prior work, and precision costs computation. Case study: commercial CMS, 62 versions, 70k-127k LOC, up to 101 tables and 568 stored procedures; a secondary summary reports analysis under 2 minutes with no false negatives and a low level of false positives; introduces a "warning" category for impacts that change behaviour without outright errors. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/19497/1/19497.pdf) ; [bibtex](https://bibtex.github.io/ICSE-2008-MauleER.html) [S; "no false negatives" is Sec]. For Teslon: schema-to-code impact can be done statically with no false negatives on one real system, but only with context-sensitive dataflow over the SQL strings; Teslon's Liquibase/Flyway reading should be judged against this bar (column -> query -> repository method -> endpoint -> screen), and dynamic SQL strings are the known failure point.
- Meurice, Nagy, Cleve (QRS 2016), "Detecting and preventing program inconsistencies under database schema evolution": mines history for inconsistencies left by past schema changes and runs what-if simulations to recommend where to propagate a change. — [PDF](https://loupmeurice.github.io/publications/QRS2016.pdf) [S]. Qiu, Li, Su (FSE 2013), "An empirical analysis of the co-evolution of schema and code in database applications" — [PDF](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~su/publications/fse13-db-study.pdf) [S, title only]; from memory [Mem], schema changes are frequent and each requires a substantial number of code changes. Lin and Neamtiu (2009), "Collateral evolution of applications and databases" — [PDF](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~neamtiu/pubs/lin09collateral.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: schema changes are frequent and their code impact is real; history (did code change along with the migration?) is a useful UNCLEAR signal.

Configuration
- See Q2: uRTS (ICSE 2023), ctest (OSDI 2020), Rex (NSDI 2020, Q4). Together they show configuration is a first-class, high-risk change type, and that the only safe treatment is to model config keys as nodes with explicit readers.


### Inferences
- Teslon's cross-stack chain (DB column -> repository -> endpoint -> HTTP client -> component -> route/screen) has no published end-to-end evaluation in the literature; each hop has partial precedent (Maule for schema, Tochal for JS events, Cerny for services) and each precedent reports specific failure points (dynamic SQL, runtime-registered events, name-based joins).
- The closest analogue to Teslon's users (Nakagawa 2019, manual E2E of commercial web apps; Elsner 2022, 20 manual E2E tests on Corona-Warn-App) used recorded traces, not static analysis, and reported effort savings, not safety bounds.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Nakagawa 2019 and Tochal 2015 were not reachable; their precision and miss numbers are missing.
- No published dataset maps code changes to affected screens or UI flows with ground truth.
- No study of Feign/Kafka-level static change impact with measured recall was found (the council's microSecEnD/Code2DFD recall numbers concern architecture extraction, not change impact).

## Q4. Industrial reports (Google, Meta, Microsoft, SAP, Ericsson, others)

### Takeaway
No large company claims a safe (zero-miss) selector in production. Every industrial selector either (a) uses a coarse, conservative build/dependency graph and still runs everything later (Google TAP, Bazel-style), or (b) knowingly accepts a measured miss rate in exchange for cost, backed by a later full or batched run that catches the misses (Meta: >99.9% of faulty changes still caught, so up to 0.1% of faulty changes escape the selected run; Microsoft FastLane: 99.99% predicted-outcome accuracy). The safety net is always a later stage that runs more tests, never the selector's own proof. Teslon's audience (manual QA) has no "later full run" behind it, so its skip promise carries more weight than any industrial selector's.

### Cited Findings
- Memon, Gao, Nguyen, Dhanda, Nickell, Siemborski, Micco (ICSE-SEIP 2017), "Taming Google-scale continuous testing", pp. 233-242, DOI 10.1109/ICSE-SEIP.2017.16. TAP is responsible for CI of most of Google's ~2 billion LOC monorepo. "Looking at the overall test history of 5.5 Million affected tests in a given time period, only 63K ever failed; the rest never failed even once" (about 1.1%). TAP "tried to test each code change, but found that the compute resources were growing quadratically", which motivated skipping tests unlikely to fail; the paper reports that test targets more than a distance of 10 in the code dependency graph from a change rarely fail (snippet cut off; exact rate not seen). — [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/taming-google-scale-continuous-testing/) ; [PDF mirror](https://huang.isis.vanderbilt.edu/cs8395-f23/paper/google-testing-icse-seip-17.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: Google's selection is the build dependency graph (conservative) plus milestone batching; the "distance" heuristic is a cost heuristic, not a safety proof; and the 98.9% never-failing tests show why selectors look good on average while the rare misses are what matter.
- Leong, Singh, Papadakis, Le Traon, Micco (ICSE-SEIP 2019), "Assessing transition-based test selection algorithms at Google", pp. 101-110. Offline replay of TAP history with flaky executions filtered; heuristics based on a test's recent affecting commits did best (beating random on >30% of transition commits in some cases); history- and transition-based heuristics did worse than expected; directory-overlap heuristic "should be discarded"; "the test selection problem remains largely open"; flaky transitions significantly distort evaluations. — [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/assessing-transition-based-test-selection-algorithms-at-google/) ; [PDF](https://mpapad.github.io/publications/pdfs/ICSE-SEIP2019.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: (1) path/directory proximity is a poor impact signal (Teslon must not fall back to "same folder" reasoning); (2) any validation on real history must filter flaky outcomes or it will mis-score both misses and catches.
- Machalica, Samylkin, Porth, Chandra (ICSE-SEIP 2019), "Predictive test selection" (Facebook/Meta), arXiv 1810.05286. Learned from historical outcomes; "halves the infrastructure cost of testing changes" while guaranteeing that "over 95% of individual test failures and over 99.9% of faulty changes are still reported back to developers"; deployed more than a year; failed tests retried aggressively in training data to handle flakiness. — [ICSE program page](https://2019.icse-conferences.org/details/icse-2019-Software-Engineering-in-Practice/22/Predictive-Test-Selection) ; [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05286) ; [Meta engineering blog](https://code.fb.com/?p=11039) [S]. For Teslon: the most-cited industrial selector openly accepts losing up to 5% of individual test failures and up to 0.1% of faulty changes at the selection stage. This is the industry's accepted miss budget for CI with a later safety net; it is NOT an acceptable budget for a tool that tells manual testers "skip, proven".
- Philip, Bhagwan, Kumar, Maddila, Nagappan (ICSE 2019, Microsoft), "FastLane: test minimization for rapidly deployed large-scale online services", pp. 408-418, DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2019.00054. ML on test and commit logs predicts test outcomes (and commit risk); tests with predicted outcomes are not run; "saves 18.04%, i.e., almost a fifth of test-time while obtaining a test outcome accuracy of 99.99%"; offers threshold knobs trading time saved against accuracy. — [MSR PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/12/PID5783051.pdf) ; [ICSE page](https://2019.icse-conferences.org/details/icse-2019-Technical-Papers/14/FastLane-Test-Minimization-for-Rapidly-Deployed-Large-scale-Online-Services) [S]. For Teslon: a 99.99% outcome accuracy on predicted tests still means 1 in 10,000 predicted outcomes is wrong; at Microsoft scale that was accepted because of later safety nets. Commit-risk classification ("major change = risky") is a useful ordering idea for Teslon's TEST list, not for SKIP.
- Mehta, Bhagwan, Kumar, Bansal, Maddila, Ashok, Asthana, Bird, Kumar (NSDI 2020, Microsoft), "Rex: preventing bugs and misconfiguration in large services using correlated change analysis". Learns change rules (association rules over months of file changes plus program analysis of config) and warns when a file that usually changes with the edited ones was not changed; deployed 14 months on 360 repositories (Exchange Online, OneDrive, Azure, Dynamics CRM, Skype, Office 365); "positively affected 4926 changes". — [USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/mehta) ; [PDF](https://usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-mehta.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: industrial validation of the council's "co-change guard / rule-gap miner": correlated-change rules catch config and code mismatches static readers miss. Adopt as an UNCLEAR-raising signal; Rex's design shows it can be made offline and deterministic (association rules, no LLM).
- Hammant (2017), "The Rise of Test Impact Analysis", martinfowler.com, 22 Aug 2017. Describes TIA as building a map from tests to production code via coverage/instrumentation during test runs; credits Microsoft with the longest effort to productionise TIA and with naming it; notes teams can implement something useful cheaply. — [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/rise-test-impact-analysis.html) [S]. For Teslon: the industry meaning of "TIA" is dynamic (coverage-based) and test-centric; Teslon is static and area-centric, and should say so plainly to avoid being judged as a weaker TIA.
- Microsoft Azure DevOps TIA (2017 blog series "Accelerated Continuous Testing with Test Impact Analysis", parts 1-4): TIA runs in CI and PR workflows and "will automatically select only the subset required to validate the code being committed". — [Microsoft DevOps blog, part 2, 16 May 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/devops/accelerated-continuous-testing-with-test-impact-analysis-part-2/) [S]. From memory [Mem]: the same series lists cases where TIA falls back to running all tests (e.g. changes to non-code files it cannot map, first run without a baseline), and the docs offered a periodic "run all tests" override; not re-checked here. For Teslon: even Microsoft's product keeps a periodic full-run override, i.e. the vendor does not trust the map alone.
- Bach, Andrzejak, Pannemans (ICST Workshops 2017), "Coverage-based reduction of test execution time: lessons from a very large industrial project" (SAP HANA, multi-million-line C/C++). Overlap-aware selection achieves "up to 50% higher code coverage in a fixed time budget"; named obstacles: large amounts of shared, non-specific coverage between tests, high redundancy of coverage data, and randomness (flakiness) of test results. — [PDF](https://aip.ifi.uni-heidelberg.de/fileadmin/papers/2017/Coverage-Based_Reduction_of_Test_Execution_Time_icst2017.pdf) [S]. Bach's 2020 Heidelberg PhD thesis covers testing in very large projects: [dissertation PDF](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/31757/7/dissertation-tbach-color-druck.pdf) [S, title only]. For Teslon: in large systems most code is "shared" (touched by almost every test or screen); a change in shared code makes every area TEST. Teslon should expect high TEST/UNCLEAR rates on shared-library changes and present them as honest, not as a defect.


- Shopify Engineering, "Spark joy by running fewer tests" (blog, about 2021 [Mem for year]): static dependency-graph test selection on the Ruby monolith reported 99.94% recall of failures, and says the team resolved most root causes of the missed failures; considered Sorbet types for the graph but that needed the whole codebase in strict Sorbet. — [Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/spark-joy-by-running-fewer-tests) [S]. For Teslon: the clearest public number for a static selector in a large real codebase: about 6 in 10,000 failures missed, and the root causes were fixed one by one. A static tool's misses are a backlog to burn down, which matches the council's "unknown ledger" and rule-gap miner.
- Stripe, "Selective test execution at Stripe: dynamic discovery and verification at massive scale" (50M-line Ruby monorepo; only a third-party repost was found, technical details not visible). — [repost](https://dyb.mataroa.blog/blog/selective-test-execution-at-stripe-dynamic-discovery-and-verification-at-massive-scale/) [S; original on stripe.dev not reached]. For Teslon: title signals Stripe pairs selection with "verification", i.e. a check that selection did not miss; read the original before citing.
- Ericsson: a Chalmers MSc thesis built a framework to evaluate RTS on data from an Ericsson site; history-based selection beat random selection in fail detection — [Chalmers ODR](https://odr.chalmers.se/items/1228546e-e438-4fbf-b705-160ee5dcacd2/full) [S]; Engstroem, Runeson et al. (ST-Ericsson, ICST 2011), "Improving regression testing transparency and efficiency with history-based prioritization - an industrial case study": tool-based (fix-cache) selection gave more efficient suites than manual, experience-based selection — [Lund record](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/lup/publication/147aa503-f71d-474a-a7e0-f2a0d8fcbecc) [S]; another report states Ericsson's large-scale regression suites were selected manually by experts based on experience [S, same search]. For Teslon: telecom-scale industry still relies on expert manual selection for system tests; the opportunity is real, and tool support beat expert selection in the one comparison found.
- Di Nardo, Alshahwan, Briand, Labiche (STVR 2015), "Coverage-based regression test case selection, minimization and prioritization: a case study on an industrial system": selection gave no significant savings in execution cost (under 2%) on that system; finer-grained coverage helped prioritisation. — [UL record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/coverage-based-regression-test-case-selection-minimization-and-pr/) [S]. For Teslon: on some industrial systems almost every test touches the change, and selection buys nothing; expect high TEST rates on such apps and do not oversell skip counts.
- Uber, Spotify, Dropbox, Salesforce: no primary engineering-blog or paper on test selection miss rates was found in this run [gap].

### Inferences
- Industry's accepted miss budgets (Meta up to 0.1% of faulty changes, FastLane 1 in 10,000 predicted outcomes, Shopify 0.06% of failures, Teamscale 10% of failed builds in the fast subset) all sit on top of a later, fuller run. Teslon's output to manual testers has no such backstop, so Teslon must hold itself to a stricter standard than any of these, or ship SKIP as "deprioritise" rather than "skip".
- The pattern that recurs at Google, Shopify and Microsoft is: coarse conservative graph, measured misses, each miss root-caused and fixed. That is an achievable launch posture for Teslon ("we publish every wrong skip and its fix"), whereas "never a wrong skip" is not demonstrable.
- Directory proximity (Google) and generic history heuristics do poorly; dependency structure is what works, which supports Teslon's static-graph approach.

### Gaps
- Memon 2017's failure rate for targets beyond distance 10, and Microsoft's TIA fall-back rules, were not visible.
- No primary source on Uber, Spotify, Dropbox, Salesforce or SAP (beyond HANA coverage work) test-selection miss rates was found.
- Stripe's original post was not reached.

## Q5. Manual and system-level test selection, test-gap analysis

### Takeaway
The only sustained research line aimed at manual testers is the TUM/CQSE/Saarland "test gap analysis" (TGA) work (2011-2025). Its core empirical finding is the strongest argument for Teslon's existence: in a structured industrial process, about half of changes reached production untested, and untested changes carried up to five times more defects. But every TGA result relies on recorded coverage of manual tests (a running, instrumented app), and none claims a "safe to skip" verdict; TGA says "this changed code was not executed by any test", which is a gap list, not a skip list. No paper found evaluates a static, no-execution tool that tells manual testers what to skip.

### Cited Findings
- Juergens, Hummel, Deissenboeck, Feilkas, Schloegel, Wuebbeke (CSMR 2011, industrial track), "Regression test selection of manual system tests in practice", pp. 309-312, DOI 10.1109/CSMR.2011.44. Industrial case study of how regression selection for manual system tests performs in practice, with proposals to improve it; argues prior RTS evaluations covered only automated tests, while manual system tests usually cover large parts of the system end to end. — [Teamscale-hosted PDF](https://teamscale.com/hubfs/26978363/Publications/2011-regression-test-selection-of-manual-system-tests-in-practice.pdf) ; [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/csmr/JurgensHDFSW11.html) [S]. Exact percentages of changed code missed by testers' manual selection were not visible in the snippet [gap]. For Teslon: this is the founding paper for Teslon's exact problem (which manual system tests to rerun for a change); its observation that one manual test covers large parts end to end means area-level (screen/flow) granularity, like Teslon's, is the right unit, and also that skipping is rarely possible for shared flows.
- Eder, Hauptmann, Junker, Juergens, Vaas, Prommer (AST 2013), "Did we test our changes? Assessing alignment between tests and development in practice", pp. 107-110, DOI 10.1109/IWAST.2013.6595800. 14-month study of an industrial business information system (about 340k LOC C#), joining development changes, test activity (manual and automated coverage) and field faults; detects methods changed but never executed in testing. As summarised by the 2025 TSE paper below: "approximately half of the changes went into production untested, and ... untested changes contained up to five times more defects than other parts of the system." — [TUM PDF](https://wwwbroy.in.tum.de/publ/papers/AST13Hauptmann.pdf) ; [secondary summary in Haas et al. 2025](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/HSJ+25.pdf) [Sec for "half" and "five times"; the 2013 snippet itself only shows that field bugs are substantially more likely in changed-but-untested methods]. For Teslon: the headline number for the pitch to test managers ("half of changes ship untested; those carry up to 5x defects") comes from one 340k-LOC system in 2013; cite it as one industrial case, not as a general rate.
- Rott, Niedermayr, Juergens, Pagano (WETSoM 2017; arXiv 1804.07599, 2018), "Ticket coverage: putting test coverage into context". Per-ticket ratio of changed methods covered by automated or manual tests; industrial study on a 650k-LOC Java system found it reveals relevant test gaps. Vendor slides (not the paper) report one study of 54 tickets where testers found 110 test gaps, 20 rated critical, and a C# system where 30 of 95 analysed methods had test gaps, 23 relevant. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07599) ; [Teamscale slides, QS-Tag 2017](https://teamscale.com/fileadmin/content/news/talks/_archive/vortrag-ticket-coverage-qs-tag-2017/slides.pdf) [S; slide numbers are vendor-reported]. For Teslon: "ticket coverage" is the test-lead framing Teslon's report should mirror (per PR or per ticket: what changed, what was tested, what is a gap), and the 20-of-110 "critical" rate shows most gaps are judged low-risk by testers, so Teslon needs a risk ordering inside TEST.
- Juergens, Pagano, Goeb (CQSE whitepaper / book chapter, 2018), "Test impact analysis: detecting errors early despite large, long-running test suites". "In our studies, we were able to spot 90% of failed builds in 2% of the test execution time"; slides: 80% of faulty builds in 1% of test time. — [Teamscale PDF](https://teamscale.com/publications/2018-test-impact-analysis-detecting-errors-early-despite-large-long-running-test-suites.pdf) ; [slides](https://teamscale.com/fileadmin/content/news/talks/_archive/vortrag-test-impact-analyse-accelerate-2018/slides.pdf) [S, vendor-authored]. For Teslon: coverage-based TIA's own vendor reports 10% of failed builds NOT caught by the fast selected subset; they rely on the remaining suite running later. Again: selection is about early feedback, never about permanent skipping.
- Haas, Noemmer, Juergens, Apel (IEEE TSE 2024; ICSE 2025 journal-first), "Optimization of automated and manual software tests in industrial practice: a survey and historical analysis", DOI 10.1109/TSE.2024.3418191. Five industrial subjects in five domains; TIA and "Pareto testing" evaluated on 2,622 real historical failures; for manual test suites, average time saving of 43% (detection rate reported in paper, not visible in snippet); all five partners adopted TIA or Pareto testing; "coming with process-related trade-offs". — [Teamscale PDF](https://teamscale.com/hubfs/Publications/2024-optimization-of-automated-and-manual-software-tests.pdf) ; [ICSE 2025 page](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2025/icse-2025-journal-first-papers/28/Optimization-of-Automated-and-Manual-Software-Tests-in-Industrial-Practice-A-Survey-) [S]. For Teslon: this is the methodological template Teslon should copy for its own validation: replay a selector over real historical failures from industrial partners (2,622 failures across 5 subjects) and report the detection rate. It is also the closest published evidence that manual-test selection is adoptable.
- Haas, Sailer, Joblin, Juergens, Apel (IEEE TSE 2025), "Prioritizing test gaps by risk in industrial practice: an automated approach and multimethod study", DOI 10.1109/TSE.2025.3556248. "Untested code changes, called test gaps, pose a significant risk"; "even a lightweight prioritization approach helps practitioners to identify high-risk test gaps efficiently and to filter out low-risk test gaps." Haas's Saarland PhD dissertation (defended 17 Sep 2025) covers the same theme; on historical QA documents from industry partners, the approach "prioritized risky changes significantly higher". — [Saarland PDF](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/HSJ+25.pdf) ; [dissertation record](https://jahrbib.sulb.uni-saarland.de/handle/20.500.11880/41110?locale=en) [S]. For Teslon: risk ordering of TEST areas is accepted and evaluated in industry; it is low-risk to adopt because ordering cannot create a wrong skip.
- CQSE vendor claim: test gap analysis "reduces the number of errors in the field by as much as 50%" in one customer study; Teamscale pages claim "find 90% of the defects with only 2% of the test runtime" (TIA) or "11%" (Pareto). — [Eurostar talk page](https://conference.eurostarsoftwaretesting.com/?p=54486) ; [Teamscale test selection page](https://teamscale.com/test-selection) [S; vendor claims, unverified]. For Teslon: these are the numbers a buyer will compare Teslon against; Teslon cannot quote anything comparable until it runs on a real team's history.


### Inferences
- Test gap analysis and Teslon answer different questions: TGA lists changed code that no test executed (needs a running, instrumented app); Teslon lists areas to test or skip without running anything. They are complementary: TGA's per-test coverage would be an independent oracle for Teslon's skips (the council's R6 runtime layer), and Teslon could give TGA a "where to look" layer for gaps.
- The test-manager pitch ("half of changes ship untested; they carry up to five times more defects") rests on one 2013 C# system; it is persuasive but must be cited as a single case.
- The Haas 2024 design (historical failures from industrial partners, detection rate, time saved, adoption) is the most directly copyable validation protocol for Teslon's first enterprise pilot.

### Gaps
- The 2011 Juergens paper's quantitative results (how much changed code testers' manual selection missed) were not visible.
- The detection rate for manual suites in Haas 2024 was not visible (only the 43% average time saving).
- No study of a static, execution-free tool for manual testers was found.

## Q6. LLM-era work 2023-2026

### Takeaway
The only controlled measurement of an LLM doing code-level change impact analysis against a manually labelled ground truth (Stengg, Macho, Pinzger, Dec 2025) found GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini performed poorly, with median precision, recall and F1 of 0.0 in the base setting; adding diff hunks lifted GPT-5's third-quartile F1 only to about 0.4. Newer research (Ripple, ICSE 2026) uses LLMs only to refine a structurally computed impact set. LLM-based PR risk scoring works as a ranking signal in industry (Meta: gating the top 10% riskiest diffs captured over 40% of outages), never as a safety proof. No benchmark exists with ground truth for "what should be tested for this PR" at the screen/flow level. This supports Teslon's choice of no LLM in any decision, and also means "deterministic" is a differentiator worth stating with these numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Stengg, Macho, Pinzger (arXiv 2512.19481, 22 Dec 2025), "A dataset and preliminary study of using GPT-5 for code-change impact analysis". Dataset "Alextend": extends ALEXANDRIA (method-level Java changes, which lacked seed-change information); 40 commits, 192 code-change pairs, 14 popular Java GitHub projects, randomly selected; ground truth = all direct and indirect dependent code entities of the seed changes, identified manually; scored by exact fully-qualified-name match per commit. GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini "both performed poorly"; median precision, recall and F1 of 0.0; with diff hunks GPT-5's Q3 F1 rose to 0.4 and GPT-5-mini's to 0.04. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19481) ; [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.19481v1) ; [authors' PDF](https://pinzger.github.io/papers/Stengg2025-ciadataset.pdf) [S; the 0.0 medians and Q3 0.4 come from a search summary of the paper, not read in full]. For Teslon: the best 2025 general model cannot reproduce even direct+indirect dependency sets reliably; an LLM may phrase test steps, but any LLM-produced edge must be checked by the analyzer (as the council decided). Also a small public ground-truth CIA dataset Teslon could reuse for Java method-level sanity checks.
- Yadavally and Nguyen (ICSE 2026), "From seed to scope: reasoning to identify change impact sets" (Ripple), DOI 10.1145/3744916.3773265. Argues structural and co-change analyses produce large impact sets with low precision; phase 1 expands seeds via evolutionary and dependence coupling (for recall), phase 2 uses an LLM-generated change plan to refine (for precision); repository slicing to reduce hallucination. — [authors' PDF](https://aashishyadavally.github.io/assets/pdf/pub-icse2026-(2).pdf) [S; evaluation numbers not visible]. For Teslon: the 2026 state of the art keeps LLMs downstream of a structural over-approximation, which is the same layering as Teslon's "LLM proposes, analyzer verifies" rule; but Ripple uses the LLM to remove items (precision), which would be a wrong-skip risk in Teslon's setting.
- Vision paper arXiv 2606.18855 (2026): proposes semantic similarity plus graph propagation with a tunable precision/recall weight; authors say prototype numbers are "illustrative of the mechanism, not a generalization claim". — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18855) [S]. For Teslon: no evidence value.
- Requirements-level LLM CIA (arXiv 2511.00262, Oct 2025, ProReFiCIA): recall 93.3% on a benchmark and 95.8% on a new industry dataset. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00262v1) [S]. For Teslon: requirements-level, not code; shows recall-oriented reporting is possible but not comparable.
- Test selection with LLMs: TestPrune (arXiv 2510.18270, Oct 2025) uses an LLM to predict suspicious methods from an issue description and prefers tests covering them; evaluated for issue repair on SWE-Bench at $0.02-$0.05 per instance with GPT-4o and Claude-3.7-Sonnet; it is about repair success, not selection safety. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18270v1) [S]. A 2025 survey of agentic issue resolution argues LLM-based regression test selection is unsound in both directions because selection is a model judgment rather than a dependency analysis. — [arXiv 2512.22256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.22256) [S]. "Faster feedback with AI? A test prioritization study" (Programming 2024): evaluates LLM and embedding models on nominating failing tests for fault-injected real changes; concludes "the high computational cost of AI models is not always justified, and tool developers should also consider non-AI models". — [conference page](https://2024.programming-conference.org/details/programming-2024-programming-with-ai/4/Faster-Feedback-with-AI-A-Test-Prioritization-Study) [S]. For Teslon: no peer-reviewed evidence that LLM test selection is safe; the evidence points the other way.
- Benchmarks with "what to test" ground truth: none at screen/flow level. Nearest: SWE-fficiency (arXiv 2511.06090) labels relevant tests by coverage intersection with modified lines ("a conservative form of test-impact analysis"); TeleSWEBench (arXiv 2606.05001) maps tests to changed implementation files. — [SWE-fficiency](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.06090) ; [TeleSWEBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.05001) [S]. For Teslon: Teslon could publish the first screen/API/job-level PR impact benchmark; that would itself be a "1 of 1" asset.
- PR risk prediction: Meta's Diff Risk Score (2026 preliminary study, arXiv 2607.02782 and earlier work) "capturing over 40% of outages by gating just the top 10% of diffs by risk", validated against expert-annotated outage root causes; DRS-OSS (arXiv 2511.21964, fine-tuned Llama 3.1 8B) claims it "could prevent up to 86.4% of defect-inducing changes" (conditions unclear); Amazon deployment-risk work reports F1 0.846 with LLMs as feature extractors. — [alphaXiv 2607.02782](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.02782) ; [DRS-OSS](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21964v2) ; [Amazon PDF](https://cdn.amazon.science/68/86/8e1ed4ef4ac9bffbae32ff718b99/deployment-risk-assessment-camera-ready-camera-ready.pdf) [S; all self-reported by the builders]. For Teslon: risk scores catch a minority of outages at a fixed budget (40% at top 10%); fine for ordering TEST areas, unusable for SKIP.
- Commercial LLM test selection: Momentic "AI Select" docs say benchmark results are not guarantees and that when it cannot build a safe selection it runs the full in-scope set. — [Momentic docs](https://momentic.ai/docs/ai/select) [S; vendor]. PruneTest (GitHub Action) combines static import analysis, embeddings and a GBDT model, LLM only for an ambiguous band. — [GitHub](https://github.com/cjlee1/PruneTest) [S]. For Teslon: even LLM-first vendors fall back to "run everything" when unsure; that is the industry's version of Teslon's UNCLEAR.


### Inferences
- The evidence base for LLM change impact analysis is small but negative: median F1 0.0 on 40 labelled commits. Teslon's "no LLM in any decision" is supported by evidence, and is a selling point in regulated enterprises.
- LLMs are being used in research to cut impact sets for precision (Ripple), which is precisely the operation that creates wrong skips in Teslon's setting. If Teslon ever adds an LLM, it may only add candidate areas or phrase checks, never remove areas.
- There is no ground-truth benchmark for "what should be tested for this PR" at screen/API level; building one is an open opportunity.

### Gaps
- Ripple's (ICSE 2026) numbers were not visible.
- Meta DRS details are from a 2026 preprint summary; not peer-reviewed in what was seen.
- No study measuring LLM hallucination rate or run-to-run reproducibility in change impact analysis specifically was found; reproducibility claims for LLM tools remain unmeasured in this literature.

## Q8. Evaluation methodology, and the evidence standard for "safe to skip"

### Takeaway
The community accepts two kinds of proof that a selector is safe, and needs both: (1) an analytical argument that the selector is safe under explicitly stated assumptions (Rothermel and Harrold 1996), and (2) empirical checks that the assumptions hold on real code, done by comparing against a reference that runs more (RetestAll or a dynamic selector), by rule-based differential checking of generated evolutions (RTSCheck), by mutants (with the known coupling gap), and by replaying real historical failures with flaky outcomes filtered. Every ground-truth source has a documented bias. Statistically, zero observed misses in n independent skip decisions only bounds the miss rate below about 3/n at 95% confidence, so "100% without mistakes" can never be shown by testing (Butler and Finelli 1993); it can only be argued by proof plus printed assumptions, with a measured upper bound alongside.

### Cited Findings
How ground truth for impact is built (and each method's bias)
- Same-commit co-change as truth (ROSE, Hassan-Holt, TARMAQ). Rolfsnes, Di Alesio, Behjati, Moonen, Binkley (SANER 2016), "Generalizing the analysis of evolutionary coupling for software change impact analysis" (TARMAQ): beats ROSE and SVD on six systems, "applicable 100% of the time", orders of magnitude faster than SVD; truth = other entities changed in the same transaction. — [Simula page](https://www.simula.no/research/generalizing-analysis-evolutionary-coupling-software-change-impact-analysis) [S]. Bias (inference): co-change truth only contains what developers edited, never entities whose behaviour changed without being edited, which is exactly what QA must test; tangled commits add noise.
- Manually untangled commits: Yan, Cooper, Moran, Bavota, Poshyvanyk, Rich (FSE 2024), ATHENA, "leveraging call graphs to improve impact analysis", with the ALEXANDRIA benchmark: 910 commits across 25 open-source Java projects, 4,405 impact-analysis tasks, built from manually untangled bug-fix commits; each changed method is a query and the remaining co-changed methods are the truth set. — [FSE 2024 PDF](https://www.cs.wm.edu/~denys/pubs/_FSE_24__Athena__Leveraging_Call_Graphs_Improve_Impact_Analysis.pdf) ; [Zenodo / arXiv 2607.23355 extended version](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.23355) [S]. Stengg et al. 2025 criticise ALEXANDRIA for having no clear seed change and extend it with seeds (Alextend, see Q6). For Teslon: the largest public CIA benchmark is method-level co-change for Java libraries; nothing at screen/API level.
- Developer or expert labels: Toth et al. (PPPJ 2010) found programmers' impact sets disagreed with all four algorithms (Q1); Alextend (2025) uses manual labels on 40 commits. Bias: small, expensive, and raters disagree; needs adjudication.
- Execution differencing (semantic dependence) as truth: Cai and Santelices (JSS 2015) (Q1). Bias: truth depends on the inputs executed; an impact not exercised by the inputs is invisible.
- Mutation as truth: Musco et al. (2017) (Q1); commit-relevant mutants have "30% higher fault revelation ability (w.r.t. real commit-introduced faults)" than other strategies for the same number of mutants (EMSE 2022). — [Springer EMSE 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-022-10138-1) [S]. Just et al. (FSE 2014): mutants are a valid substitute for real faults in most cases but about 17% of real faults coupled to no mutant [Sec: council R7]. A Defects4J-based study found real faults harder for reordered suites to detect than mutants and recommends evaluating with both. — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/131042/1/tcp.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: mutants (Teslon's 434 compiler-proven mutants) are necessary but not sufficient; at least a sixth of real fault types will not be represented.
- Real historical failures: Haas et al. (TSE 2024) evaluated selection on 2,622 real failures from five industrial histories (Q5); Leong et al. (2019) replayed TAP history with flaky executions filtered (Q4); Machalica et al. (2019) used historical outcomes with aggressive retries to remove flakiness (Q4). For Teslon: replaying real CI/QA failures is the accepted industrial evidence; it requires access to a partner's history.
- SZZ (bug-introducing commits traced back from bug-fixing commits): Herbold, Trautsch, Trautsch, Ledel (EMSE 2022, vol. 27, art. 42), "Problems with SZZ and features": on 398 releases of 38 Apache projects, "only half of the bug fixing commits determined by SZZ are actually bug fixing"; SZZ misses about one fifth of bug-fixing commits (wrong issue links, mislabeled issues); with a six-month window, one file is wrongly labelled defective for every correctly labelled one, and two defective files are missed. — [Goettingen PDF](https://publications.goettingen-research-online.de/bitstream/2/98985/1/document.pdf) ; [arXiv 1911.08938](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1911.08938) [S]. For Teslon: the council's "SZZ backtest" idea (I2) is valid only with manual validation of every candidate wrong skip; raw SZZ output would be half noise.

How RTS safety is evaluated
- Reference comparison: tests selected by RetestAll or by a dynamic selector (Ekstazi) but not by the static one count as safety violations (Legunsen et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2019; Q2). Rule-based differential checking on generated program evolutions (RTSCheck, Zhu et al. 2019: 27 tool bugs; Q2). Change-kind catalogues validated on 1,150 versions of 23 projects (Liu et al. 2023; Q2).
- Seeded or real faults: classical studies on small seeded-fault programs (Siemens suite era, Rothermel and Harrold 1997-1998 [Mem]); modern studies use real CI failures and mutants.

Statistics of "zero misses"
- Rule of three: if 0 events occur in n independent trials, the upper 95% confidence limit on the event rate is about 3/n (0 in 50 -> up to 6%; 0 in 1,000 -> up to 0.3%); for 99% confidence use 4.61/n. Hanley and Lippman-Hand (JAMA 1983, 249(13):1743-5), "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators". — [JAMA abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/389714) ; [summary with derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(statistics)) [S/Sec]. For Teslon: 55 live PRs with 0 wrong skips bounds the wrong-skip rate only below about 5.5% (3/55) at 95%, and only for PRs like those (own fixtures, same authors). The 60+60 history scans checked citations and crashes, not skip correctness, so they bound nothing about wrong skips.
- Butler and Finelli (IEEE TSE 1993, 19(1):3-12), "The infeasibility of quantifying the reliability of life-critical real-time software": classical statistical estimation leads to "exorbitant amounts of testing" for ultra-reliability; reliability-growth models cannot avoid it; independence of separately built versions cannot be justified experimentally in that region. — [NASA NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040139817/downloads/20040139817.pdf) [S]. For Teslon: "100% without mistakes" is not a demonstrable empirical claim; the defensible claim is "safe under these printed assumptions, with an independent checker, and a measured wrong-skip upper bound of X% at 95% on N independent decisions per stratum". The second-version argument (Teslon's independent checker) also cannot be assumed to fail independently of the engine if both encode the same framework model; independence must be designed (different authors, different fact sources).

### Known failure modes of static impact analysis
(Each item: what breaks, and the source that measured or documented it. [S] = abstract/snippet level, [Sec] = secondary, [Mem] = recalled.)
1. Reflection and string-based lookup (Class.forName, method lookups, DI by name): the only cause of unsafety in static class-level RTS on 22 Java projects; making it safe erased most savings. — Legunsen et al. FSE 2016; Shi et al. OOPSLA 2019 [S/Sec] (Q2).
2. Fine granularity: method-level static RTS "performed rather poorly"; finer claims need more justification than class/module-level ones. — Legunsen et al. 2016 [S] (Q2).
3. Framework callbacks and dependency injection not in the call graph (container-invoked beans, AOP proxies, event listeners, scheduled jobs): classical safe RTS assumes a complete program graph including libraries. — Harrold et al. OOPSLA 2001 [Mem]; Rothermel and Harrold 1996 [S] (Q2).
4. Configuration changes and code+config co-changes: no RTS before 2023 reasoned about both; faulty configs were the second-largest cause of disruptions in one major Google service. — uRTS ICSE 2023; ctest OSDI 2020 [S] (Q2).
5. Correlated non-code files that must change together (config, deployment, schema), invisible to code readers but caught by co-change rules (4,926 changes affected in 14 months). — Rex NSDI 2020 [S] (Q4).
6. Dependency/library version bumps: client sets reach hundreds of thousands; industry stays at project-level selection. — Gyori et al. ISSRE 2018 [S] (Q2); Teslon's own 295fa8d example [Sec: PRIOR-ART.md].
7. Database schema changes reached through SQL strings: need context-sensitive dataflow; dynamic SQL defeats it. — Maule et al. ICSE 2008 [S] (Q3).
8. Client-side JavaScript events, DOM-registered handlers and asynchronous client-server calls: static-only JS impact analysis misses them. — Alimadadi et al. ECOOP 2015 [Mem] (Q3).
9. Cross-process and cross-service calls: dynamic RTS is limited to one process; static joins across services are prototype-stage. — Elsner et al. AST 2022 [S]; Cerny et al. 2025 [S] (Q3, Q7).
10. Harmless-looking changes that change behaviour (constants, defaults, annotations, ordering): 11 of 13 harmless-looking cases in the FineRTS study were behaviour-changing. — Liu et al. ISSTA 2023 [Sec: council R3] (Q2).
11. Tool implementation bugs: 27 bugs (23 new) in Ekstazi, STARTS and Clover. — Zhu et al. ICSE 2019 [Sec] (Q2).
12. Shared code: in large systems most coverage is shared and non-specific, so changes in shared code select almost everything. — Bach et al. SAP HANA 2017 [S] (Q4).
13. Impacts without edits: co-change-based truth and history miners only see what developers edited; behaviour changes in unedited entities are missed by history-based CIA. — Zimmermann et al. 2004 recall of 26% of files [S] (Q1); inference.
14. Low recall of every single CIA technique against independent truth: dynamic CIA 38-56% precision/recall vs execution differencing; developer opinion disagrees with all algorithms. — Cai and Santelices JSS 2015 [S]; Toth et al. PPPJ 2010 [S] (Q1).
15. Flaky tests and non-deterministic environments: violate the controlled-regression-testing assumption; 38 of 39 missed CI failures were flaky; flaky transitions distort evaluations. — Shi et al. ISSRE 2019 [Sec]; Leong et al. 2019 [S] (Q2, Q4).
16. Directory/path proximity as an impact proxy: performs like the weakest heuristics and "should be discarded". — Leong et al. ICSE-SEIP 2019 [S] (Q4).
17. LLM reasoning in place of analysis: median F1 0.0 for GPT-5 on labelled CIA. — Stengg et al. 2025 [S] (Q6).
18. Human manual selection (the baseline Teslon replaces): unsafe in 27% of sessions. — Gligoric et al. ASE 2014 [S] (Q7).

### What evidence a "safe to skip" claim needs
1. A stated safety condition per skip, in the Rothermel-Harrold sense: the skip holds only if listed assumptions hold (profiles and deployment, dependency versions, no unmodelled reflection/DI/AOP site on the path, config keys resolved, deterministic data). Print them with the skip. [Rothermel and Harrold 1996]
2. Coarse-first: skips proved at module/class/file level are the evidence-backed floor; finer skips (one endpoint or one screen inside a changed module) need a change-kind proof from a closed, tested catalogue (FineRTS pattern), not reachability alone. [Legunsen 2016; Liu 2023]
3. Reference comparison on real code: run a "run-more" reference (RetestAll, or coverage of the app's E2E/manual tests) on the same PRs and count skipped areas where the reference saw a difference. [Legunsen 2016; Shi 2019]
4. Rule-based differential checking of the engine and the checker on generated evolutions (RTSCheck-style), because tool bugs are a measured miss source. [Zhu 2019]
5. Independent truth sources, each with its known bias: mutants (but about 17% of real faults are not coupled to any mutant), real historical failures from a partner (Haas 2024 used 2,622), manually validated SZZ candidates (raw SZZ is about half wrong), and QA-labelled PRs with adjudication of disagreements. [Just 2014; Haas 2024; Herbold 2022; Toth 2010]
6. Flaky filtering in every history-based measurement (retries; base run three times), or noise will count as misses. [Leong 2019; Machalica 2019; Shi 2019]
7. Sample sizes from the rule of three, per stratum (stack x change kind): 0 wrong skips in 300 independent skip decisions -> wrong-skip rate below 1% at 95%; in 3,000 -> below 0.1%; at 99% confidence multiply n by about 1.54 (4.61/3). Teslon's current 55 live PRs bound the rate only below about 5.5%, and only on its own fixtures. [Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983]
8. Independence of the checker must be engineered (different authors, different fact sources, e.g. compiler/SCIP facts vs Teslon's tokenizer), because two versions built from the same model do not fail independently. [Butler and Finelli 1993]
9. Report three numbers, never one: wrong-skip rate (with its 95% upper bound), extra-test rate, and abstention (UNCLEAR) rate, each per stratum; a selector that holds everything is trivially safe and useless. [Hattori 2008 precision/recall framing; inference]
10. Compare against the human baseline on the same PRs: testers' own unaided selection was unsafe in 27% of sessions in the only field study; showing Teslon is safer than the status quo is a meaningful, achievable claim even before an absolute bound is tight. [Gligoric 2014]
11. Industry miss budgets for context only: Meta accepts up to 0.1% of faulty changes and 5% of failures escaping the selected set; Microsoft FastLane 99.99% predicted-outcome accuracy; Teamscale TIA 90% of failed builds in the fast subset. All of these have a later full run behind them; a manual-QA skip does not, so these budgets are an upper bound on what Teslon could ever accept, not a target. [Machalica 2019; Philip 2019; Juergens 2018]

### Techniques worth adopting (ranked by value for Teslon's launch; each traceable to a source above)
1. Change-kind catalogue for fine-grained skips (FineRTS, ISSTA 2023): admit a SKIP inside a changed module only for change kinds proven behaviour-preserving, each kind backed by probes; everything else at least UNCLEAR.
2. RTSCheck-style differential rule checking of engine and checker on generated evolutions (ICSE 2019), run in CI.
3. Correlated-change rules as an UNCLEAR-raising guard (Rex, NSDI 2020; ROSE/TARMAQ): deterministic association rules over the repo's history; show "these files changed together in k of n commits; this PR changed only some".
4. Config keys as first-class graph nodes per profile, including code+config co-changes (uRTS, ICSE 2023; ctest, OSDI 2020).
5. Schema-to-code dataflow with a "warning" class for behaviour-changing but non-breaking impacts (Maule, ICSE 2008).
6. A Legunsen/Haas-style external validation: open-source Spring+Angular/Vue apps with E2E suites (and, at a pilot, a partner's historical failures), hundreds of real commits, a run-more reference, flaky filtering, and rule-of-three bounds per stratum.
7. Test-gap style reporting for test leads: per PR or ticket, changed areas vs tested areas ("ticket coverage", Rott 2017), with risk ordering of TEST areas (Haas, TSE 2025).
8. Human-baseline comparison: measure testers' unaided selection on the same PRs (Gligoric, ASE 2014) and report Teslon's miss rate next to it.
9. Miss ledger with root causes, published (Shopify's approach): each wrong skip becomes a probe and a fixed rule.
10. Do not adopt: LLM-based pruning of impact sets (Ripple-style precision step), directory-proximity heuristics (Leong 2019), raw SZZ labels without manual validation (Herbold 2022), or ML risk scores as skip evidence (Meta DRS, FastLane).

### Inferences
- The council's lattice (SKIP < UNCLEAR < TEST; only static proof creates SKIP) is the literature's consensus position restated; no paper found argues that history, coverage or LLMs can prove a skip.
- Teslon's present evidence (own fixtures, same authors writing rules and truth) would not meet the bar any RTS paper since 2016 applies (independent open-source subjects, hundreds of revisions, a run-more reference). The cheapest credible upgrade is a Legunsen-style study: N open-source Spring/Angular apps with E2E suites, hundreds of real commits, Teslon's skips vs observed E2E/coverage differences.

### Gaps
- Exact numbers inside Legunsen 2016 (savings, number of unsafe revisions), Shi 2019 (unsafe percentage), uRTS (3.64x is from a secondary review), Memon 2017 (failure rate at distance >10) could not be read because full texts were blocked.
- No published sample-size standard specific to RTS safety was found; the rule-of-three numbers are general statistics applied by inference.
